# Target and source conditions before response access

The two measured ear spectra and two simulated ear spectra at the same source coordinate define complex vectors. For nonzero vectors, compare the squared magnitude of their determinant divided by the product of their squared norms. This loss is zero when the binaural responses agree up to a shared complex gain, and is bounded by one. At a unilateral ear null the determinant remains defined. An exact two-ear measured null does not define an observation in this space. Count it, retain its coordinate and require a separate identifiable-coverage receipt. A missing or zero candidate at a nonzero observation earns unit loss. No outcome-selected frequency or position exclusions.

A common source factor or common time shift cancels in this loss. A different microphone gain, delay or filter in each ear changes the result. The test concerns the released binaural transfer. It cannot recover an absolute source response, a directional-free microphone calibration or an independently verified physical ear phase. Truncating a filter does not commute with scalar spectral multiplication. For example, take left and right impulses [1,0] and [0,1]. Applying [1,1] to both gives [1,1,0] and [0,1,1]. Keeping only the first two samples changes their ratio, including at quarter sample rate. The common-reference argument alone cannot undo the authors’ 512-to-256 truncation or unequal effective NLMS filters.

The authors used a paired ear measurement with external gain/filter units and a single 0° foreground reference for all directions. Two radially outward recordings per direction were averaged. The 936 nominal directions correspond to separate acquisitions. The ranges within one direction share source motion and adaptive filter history. Negative-elevation recordings used a raised assembly. The registered unit of comparison must respect these directions and configurations.

Source eligibility requires both original SOFA objects, their original hashes, a unique measured/simulated geometry mapping, two receiver channels, compatible listener view/up/units, positive sample rate and source-bound handling of every delay. The exact BEM/FEM distinction matters. The authors describe reciprocal adaptive-order finite elements on their KEMAR CAD, with source in an ear canal. Any simulation polarity, symmetry or receiver inconsistency must be retained before a phase-sensitive comparison. More than one source per matched coordinate, independent per-ear rephasing, missing second simulated receiver, or a noncanonical coordinate convention creates a source qualification issue. A bin at a single-ear null does not.

If these conditions hold, compare the unmodified complex author FEM against a correction that depends on the direction from each SOFA receiver to the actual source. Geometry alone supplies these angles. This follows the established cross-ear parallax idea. The trained corrections act on the FEM response, so an angular calibration cannot be interpreted as an independently identified source, pinna or microphone filter. A head-centered correction with matched degrees of freedom and a distance-aware calibration from exactly the same training measurements are necessary comparators. Whole angular sweeps and extrapolated radial shells must remain separate from training. There is no claim of exact exterior-field continuation on the measured elevation belt.

Sources: Li et al., AES Convention 154, paper72; archived SOFA files Zenodo 7626148; SimpleFreeFieldHRIR SOFA convention; Cuevas-Rodríguez et al., 3D Tune-In Toolkit, 2019.
